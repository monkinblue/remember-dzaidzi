In college (Le Moyne, Syracuse, NY), I majored in Biology. After college, I was accepted into medical school at the U of NY State, Syracuse. I quit medical school in the Spring of '62, shortly afterword I enlisted in the Air Force and subsequently went to Officer Training School (OTS) and was commissioned as a 2nd Lieutenant on 21 December '62.

The primary reason for leaving med-school was my assessment that I would not feel comfortable in that profession. It was the correct decision, as I really enjoyed being a member of the military team and I met the goal I set for myself early in my career — attaining the rank of a "full bull" colonel.

Mom's maiden name — Pauline Perchinsky. Dad's — Martin Joseph Buchta. Mom's parent's — Michael Perchinky + Elizabeth Milo. Dad's parents — Martin Buchta, SR and Christina Vanek. They both lived on Mygatt St, Binghamton (both grandparents). Our family lived nearby (within a few blocks) at 41 Elm St.

## Page 11

- **Prompt Topic:** Ancestral homelands of parents and grandparents, reasons for immigrating, and their early community in America
- **Cross-References:** Referenced on p. 16

Neither of my grandparents were born in America. Grandpa + Grandma Buchta were born in Slovakia. They were <u>not</u> "Check" but Slovak, as my Grandma specifically mentioned! The Perchinsky's spoke of being "Russian". But my cousin Jeannie (Uncle George's daughter) did a lot of family history and found out that they were "Rusyn" — which is a mountaineous region in Ukraine. When asked where they came from by us, I believe that the Perchinsky grandparents were really saying "Rusyn" and not "Russian" — as those words sound the same. Both families settled on Mygatt St — which area was heavily populated by Slovaks — who centered on the Saints Cyril and Methodeus Church (for the Buchta's). The Perchinsky's followed the Greek Catholic Orthodox religion and worshipped at St. Michael's Greek Catholic Church on Clinton St. (This was the predominate religion in Rusyn). I think they were motivated to emigrate to America because of World War I.

## Page 14
- **Source:** `page_014.jpg`
- **Prompt Topic:** Grandparents' homes, cooking smells, traditions, and stories
- **Cross-References:** None

The only time we visited my Perchinsky grand-parents — enmass as a family — was to celebrate Greek Orthodox Christmas, held in January. I don't recall any particular smell of food, but I do recall a tradition. All the children were gathered in the kitchen, the lights were turned off, and then grandma would toss candies, nuts, and coins into the air — while us children scrambled in the dark to recover them as our prizes.

Grandma Buchta would bake chickens for Sunday lunch. She raised chickens in a coop in her backyard. On Saturday she would pick out several chickens, slice their throats, & let them run around the backya[rd] until they bled to death. Then grandma, my mom, and Aunt Helen (Uncle Mike's wife) would boil water in the cellar, de-feather the birds in boiling water, gut them, keep the hearts, gizzards, and livers — all in preparation for Sunday's lunch. I have never experienced as bad a smell as de-gutted chickens and boiled chicken feathers!

But Grandma's chickens for Sunday lunch were soooo goood! Even more so were the fried chicken livers for breakfast — which are likely a "turn off" for most folks — but which are still a delight for me! (with baco[n] + onions[)]

Regarding religion and my Mom and Dad (see p 17). When we were in grammar school, my brother Dick and I were altar boys, choir boys, and we attended St Cyril's Catholic school — so yes we practiced religion. My Dad worked for and retired from Ozalid. Initially, I think he did factory maintenance (e.g. painting), then on the assembly line as a punch press operator, then into designing the assembly of equipment. Ozalid made large machines which used chemicals to reproduce large drawings/blueprints (like big Xerox machines). Dad worked himself up from "blue colar" to management. Mom excelled as a mother / home maker.

I have no idea why I was named Robert. I would like to think that it was after some famous knight or warrier or scientist. Maybe my Mom just liked the name Bob. Afterall in my later life I played the stock market and I liked to think that I robbed a lot of rich folks — but I gave <u>most</u> of the spoils to me and my family, not to the poor.

## Page 17
- **Source:** `page_017.jpg`
- **Prompt Topic:** Saying grace, family mealtimes, and dinner table routines

My Dad was a very devout Catholic — mass every Sunday, confession quite frequently (maybe he was a big, secret sinner?), member of the Nocturnal Adoration Society (go to church in the middle of the night about once each month to pray for one hour). Conversely, my Mom did not attend church on a routine basis. Instead she was a devoted, loving mother, who if I were in charge of bestowing sainthood on a person, I would do so without a doubt upon her.

Mom made supper (now it's called dinner) every night. We would all eat as a family, except most times Mom ate last. I can never remember not having enough food — but we were individually limited to one pork chop, or one slice of cake — i.e. one of something since we were a big family.

Mom was a great baker and most suppers we would end with a delicious baked good.

The words I remember that my Dad used on occasion where: "Shut up you little shits and go to sleep. I don't want to hear another peep out of you!" ## Page 21
- **Source:** `page_021.jpg`
- **Prompt Topic:** Description of your childhood home, neighborhood, and view from the windows
- **Cross-References:** None

Our home at 41 Elm St was located in Binghamton's First Ward, a relatively poorer area of the city and an area largely populated by immigrants and their 1st + 2nd generation families. Our home was very modest — with 3 bedrooms and a <u>single</u> bathroom located on the 2nd floor. It was obviously not designed for a family of ten children, but somehow we all managed to be raised there, and happily I should add.

Just down the street from our home was the "corner store" — the corner of Elm + Mygatt streets. Mom would send one of us there to buy milk, bread, or sandwitch meats. The store owner kept an open tab on what was purchased and Mom/Dad paid toward the tab, probably monthly.

Elm St + Mygatt St. formed the boundaries of Spring Forest Cemetary. During warm months, Dick and I would often hike thru the cemetary, checking out gravesites and horse-ing around. We <u>never</u> did any damage. During winter we would sled down a hill in the cemetary which ran toward Elm. Dad and Mom are now buried in that same hill.

Elm Street was just an ordinary city street. Not a busy street, but when we played in the street we would yell "car coming" to alert us to clear out for any passing car.

My paper route for the "Binghamton Press" was up + down a portion of Elm and Oak streets. Dad would use the "profits" from my route to supplement the family income and I and Dick could keep any "tips". I had absolutely no problem with that.

As it turns out having the paper route was a "turn of fortune" because I won a full four year scholarship which covered my college room + board. Yippy Do! Had it not been for this scholarship, it would have been very difficult for me to pay for college.

Dick and I did collect a few things. Some transitory things were: flying grasshoppers, fireflys, baseball cards (for winning or losing by flipping them to match an opponent), horse chestnuts (a tree across the street), & night crawlers (to sell to Uncle Mike — not Uncle Mike Buchta — but Uncle Mike Sver — who resold them to fishermen).

But we were serious collectors of stamps and coins, particularly US coins. Since we both were paperboys, we received a lot of coins as payment for our newspapers. We bought books to hold our stamps and coins, hoping to complete the entire book of pennies, nickels, dimes, quarters, and fifty sent pieces. On rare occasions we would get an Indian Head penny or a zinc one made during WWII when copper was scarce. After I went off to college I took no more interest in these collections, which I never saw again. Later in life I accused Dick of selling them for cash — but he maintained his innocence + accused our brother Jim ?? Who knows? And we no longer care[.]

We also collected scrape metals, mostly when visible as trash on trash collection days. We would collect the metals (iron + especially aluminum, copper + brass) and sell them at a nearby junk yard for cash.

The chore I particularly remember was helping Mom by watching (baby sitting) my younger siblings. During the summer months of no school, Mom would have us help her until noon each day — then Dick & I were free to have our fun. Mom washed clothes, it seemed everyday. The washing machine was old fashioned with a roller type of clothes wringer — so she had to manually pass each item thru the wringer to remove the excess water. We had to fill (and later empty) large adjacent tubs with water so that Mom could rinse out the residual soap. Often, we had to rock the young ones to sleep in a baby carraige.

A chore which I really enjoyed was helping my Dad deliver soda to homes in the 1st Ward on Saturdays. We would load up the delivery truck at the soda factory, then spend the day delivering cases of soda or simply some large quarts of soda to the homes of regular customers. This was an extra job Dad did to earn some extra bucks. I learned to carry six quarts of soda between my fingers, three in each hand. Working with Dad made me feel like a "manly man".

## Page 28
- **Source:** `page_028.jpg`

My Dad did not have a car until the later years of my high school — so we walked everywhere — to school (in high school I biked), church, shopping, downtown, etc. I still remember grocery shopping with Dad, using a child's wagon to haul bags of groceries home.

I hated walking downtown to the dentist. At that time there was no dental anesthetic (Novacane) — so if you had a cavity/cavities, you also had to bear the pain! And drilling into the nerves really hurt. This is an aside, but one time while in the dentist chair I had to pee — so I was allowed to leave the chair. After peeing I could not zip up my fly (not sure of my age, but likely 8, 9, or 10). I opened the office door a wee bit, grabed my winter coat from the rack, and simply went home. (Mom later received a call, and I'm sure she and the dental nurse had a few laughs — but it was a big deal to me.)

We had a small backyard with a big apple tree. Dick and I loved to climb it. Our next door neighbor (the Bedurka's [?]) had a tree with delicious red cheeries. It overhanged our driveway and the falling cheeries made a mess. Dad did not appreciate the squashed red mess underfoot.

Let me begin by saying — I am a terrible swimmer. I'm not sure why. Perhaps it has to do with a near drowning when I was about 5 and Dick was 4 yrs old. We were at an Ozalid family picnic at Ansco Lake (near Binghamton), the two of us sitting on a dock, and we both jumped into the lake together for no apparent reason. I do remember blackness (as I sank) and light (as I surfaced). Dad, who was a lifeguard when he was young, rescued me and a lake lifeguard rescued Dick. I remember coughing up a lot of water. I am leary of water even today.

I really had to struggle to get my Boy Scout swimming merit badge — which was the last badge preventing me from becoming an Eagle Scout, a high achievement in scouting.

When I was in college I also made a stupid + poor decision and a dangerous one. A bunch of us from the dorm went to a nearby dam at night (including a priest from our floor — a sort of floor chaperone). I was persuaded (peer pressure) to jump in the water, in dark-ness and swim to shore. Fortunately, my dorm buddies stayed alongside me. But I was totally exhausted from my struggle to stay afloat. Had I sunk in the darkness.???
